# Images and diagrams

## Select images from the work

Inspect the user's documents, prior presentations and accessible design files. Inventory potential images by what they show, resolution, origin, process stage and intended use. Open the image itself rather than choosing from filenames alone.

Prefer:

1. Actual human observation and interview photos that show an interaction.
2. Actual team activity, including brainwriting, discussion, clustering and voting.
3. Original sketches, whiteboards, wireframes and different prototype forms.
4. Genuine user test situations, physical fit checks or observable task behavior.
5. Product and interface artifacts when the artifact itself is the subject.
6. Relevant sourced public images when the supplied material does not support the point.

Use as many relevant supplied images as the narrative benefits from. Do not add a photo simply to increase the count. Do not invent people or pass a stock workshop photo off as the user's project.

For public media, prefer primary or clearly licensed sources. Keep creator, source URL, license and the reason for using the image in a source ledger. Do not distribute the user's internal research photos as general-purpose stock assets inside future skills or templates unless requested.

## Evidence mapping

| Visual | It can show | It does not establish by itself |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Interview with two people | Listening, observation and asking about a routine | A validated conclusion or a statistically common behavior |
| Colleagues sharing sketches | Ideation and interdisciplinary collaboration | Empathy, unless the work includes understanding people |
| Clustered notes | Synthesis and grouping observations or ideas | A particular prioritization method unless documented |
| Voting dots | Shortlisting or prioritizing directions | Final product approval or feasibility |
| Rough wireframe | Making a question tangible at low cost | A tested or production-ready design |
| Working prototype | An experience that can support a realistic task | A real user test unless a person actually performed the task |
| Person wearing a headset | A physical-use situation or fit observation | A mouse or keyboard result |
| Alpha or beta artifact | A stage of implementation or a limited trial | A successful rollout, retention lift or proven demand |

Caption the activity and the learning purpose. For example, use "Colleagues compare and group ideas before choosing what to prototype" rather than "Teamwork." Where images come from different products, say they illustrate practices across projects.

## Preserve image quality

- Start from the best available original instead of enlarging a thumbnail or a screenshot of a screenshot.
- Inspect the focal region at the size used on the slide. Use `contain` for an interface whose edges and text matter; use an intentional crop for a human activity photo.
- Prefer another image, a smaller placement, or a different crop when detail is insufficient.
- Do not fabricate legible text on whiteboards or change a person's identity when enhancing a photo.
- Use the available image-editing tool if an actual enhancement is requested. State when an image is illustrative rather than documentary.

## Draw exact explanations

Use SVG or native presentation shapes for diagrams. Keep labels editable in PowerPoint where practical. Avoid generated images for exact process relationships or data.

Each process visual should help answer:

- What activity happens here?
- What input or observation does it use?
- What output or decision moves the team forward?
- Where can feedback send the team back?

### Reusable patterns

**A divergent and convergent framework:** Draw the two diamonds with enough vertical height for readable labels. Put the first problem area and second solution area in distinct but restrained accents. Use small action labels below each phase. Keep the photo-rich region above or beside the actual diagram. Show iteration without implying that every project follows a strict straight line.

**Two observations and one question:** Put two concrete observed behaviors in parallel. Use a clear connector to one shared question. Keep the question broad enough to permit several solutions. Do not imply causal or quantitative evidence that the sources do not provide.

**Workshop sequence:** Use two or three meaningful image regions, with short activity captions such as writing, sharing, grouping or selecting. State the output, for example a shortlist to prototype. Do not impose a particular workshop method on ambiguous photos.

**Prototype progression:** Use materially different forms, such as a sketch, layout alternatives and a working prototype. State the learning question for each. A higher-fidelity prototype is useful for a different question; it is not automatically better.

**Implementation and learning:** Connect a limited trial to observation, refinement and a decision about broader use. Label proposed steps as proposed. Add a feedback route to the earlier question when evidence changes the understanding of the problem.
