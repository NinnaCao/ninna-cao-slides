# Content and evidence

## Preserve meaning when improving a deck

Treat the latest approved file as authoritative. Separate the user-requested edit range from the rest of the presentation. Keep prior improvements unless the user asks to reverse them. A request for "more visual" usually means better evidence, diagrams and image composition, not removing most of the explanation.

Keep a slide-level content map with: number, speaker, purpose, headline, visible text, visual and its origin, notes, sources, timing, and protected status. Update it when the scope changes. Use it to keep all requested deliverables in step.

For a conversion, compare visible text, images and notes with the actual input version. Do not assume that a similarly named older PowerPoint represents a newer HTML deck. If a format requires a different layout, preserve the explanation and say so only when the difference affects use.

## Teach the concept before the workplace detail

Give the audience the main idea and enough context to recognize it in practice. Then apply it through activities, evidence and decisions. A teaching deck should let someone explain the framework afterward, not just remember the product name.

For a broad workplace example, establish the whole journey. Product form, software, setup, use and support may all be relevant. Introduce a physical prop or a price as a question about what people value when useful. Do not treat a premium price as evidence that Design Thinking succeeded.

Keep product-specific settings out of the main talk unless they make the user problem easier to understand. For example, "the player could not tell whether the change worked" is often more useful than a detailed explanation of a sensitivity setting.

## Reading coverage

Create a coverage map from the actual article or book. Include:

- Central concept or definition.
- Important distinctions and supporting concepts.
- Every required process or practice.
- The source's important examples and what each demonstrates.
- The connection to the workplace application.
- Source limitations, dates and original context for reported results.

Do not promise complete coverage without reading the supplied source. Put full exhibits, detailed historical evidence and reference tables after a visual appendix divider. Keep the concepts needed to understand the talk in the main sequence.

When an applied framework differs from the assigned reading, explain where the activities connect and where the models differ. Keep the reading central when the user asks to explain it.

Keep long quotations and repetitive paper citations off the speaking slides unless the user or assignment calls for them. Retain traceable sources in notes and the appendix. Preserve a short visible attribution when it is necessary to interpret a fact or credit an image.

## State the evidence level

| Level | Appropriate wording | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Documented activity | "The team interviewed five participants" when the source says so | Inventing sample sizes or methods |
| Observation | "One participant used another tool to check the result" | Presenting one observation as universal behavior |
| Interpretation | "This suggests a need for clearer feedback" | Calling the interpretation a proven fact |
| Framework application | "This activity illustrates discovery" | Claiming the source used that framework when it did not |
| Proposed step | "We could test the draft with a realistic task" | Describing the proposed test as completed |
| Illustrative example | "Imagine a connection drop during an important game" | Inventing an actual participant quote |

Use customer feedback to understand the underlying situation, need and emotion. Combine surveys and community feedback for patterns with direct interviews and observation for context. Do not turn every requested feature into a requirement. Frame a problem before selecting the solution.

Keep current practice separate from an improvement goal. A meeting about doing more discovery or better post-release monitoring is evidence of the intended approach, not proof that a mature process is already operating.

## Give numbers their context

Explain what a number measures, who or what it covers, its time period and why the comparison matters. Keep illustrative numbers visibly connected to their example. Do not drop a price, percentage or result onto a slide without a sentence that makes its role clear.

Remove precise claims when the evidence is missing, rather than inventing plausible values. Preserve uncertainty and verify time-sensitive public facts if they matter to the presentation.
